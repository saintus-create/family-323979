<Lead>
Until the late 1970s, the field of Basic Writing (BW) was usually called
Remedial Writing — a term dropped for its negative connotations. Widely
accepted alternate terms include Developmental Writing and Writing for
Underprepared Students.
</Lead>

## Who BW students are

Students in BW courses generally fall into two groups. The first consists of
students newly graduated from high school — sometimes with average or even
above-average English grades — whose scores on college placement tests are low
and who are advised or required (depending on the college's policy) to take BW.

The second, usually larger, group fits the profile of the "nontraditional
student": over twenty-five years of age; a high school graduate of more than
six years; working full-time; attending part-time; hoping to train for a new or
better job; with limited funds for books and materials; raising a family (if a
single parent, almost always the mother).

For a small but rapidly growing subset, English is not the first language.
Some, though not all, have completed ESL coursework before taking BW. The
instructional needs of ESL students in BW courses often differ from those of
native-born students linguistically as well as culturally — though when ESL
students are in the minority, instruction tends to be geared to the
native-born students.

## The writing itself

BW student writing can be described from two perspectives: typical behavior
during the writing process, and features of the writing.

Typical behavior includes avoidance of planning; seemingly torturous labor to
get even a few sentences onto paper; and over-concern with fine-tuning —
attending to a spelling or punctuation uncertainty long before sufficient
material has been drafted or revised. Such "premature editing" prevents
writers from focusing on the larger issues of developing ideas, marshaling
context and support, and arranging material effectively. The discouragement
can be so overwhelming that BW students withdraw from class — or college. For
teachers to give sufficiently individual attention, BW classes must be
reasonable in size: fifteen students per teacher is the standard in the Modern
Language Association's "ADE Guidelines for Class Size and Workload for College
and University Teachers of English."

Typical features of the writing include grammatical and punctuation errors,
and a paucity of conceptual complexity — thin or illogical reasoning, little
specific support, or unrelated conclusions. The importance assigned to these
features varies by institution type: a study of BW essays at two- and four-year
colleges found that lack of conceptual complexity, even absent grammatical
errors, often led to BW placement at four-year institutions, while at two-year
colleges it was rarely sufficient cause by itself.

## Teaching and programs

To teach BW effectively, teachers need background in linguistics, composition
theory, and learning theory; since the mid-1980s a small but growing number of
institutions require BW instructors to have taken at least one or two graduate
courses in the theory and practice of teaching BW.

Program configurations vary: some colleges offer a single one-semester course;
others a sequence, most commonly of two. The theoretically grounded
pedagogical approach is "top-down," exposing BW students at all levels to the
concept that writing is a process of making meaning rather than a collection
of atomistic skills: students write multiple drafts, practice intensive
revision, and learn to be peer critics. A growing enhancement integrates the
processes of reading with writing to encourage analysis and synthesis, often
through a crafted sequence of assignments. The alternative "bottom-up"
structure — lower levels concentrating on sentence and paragraph writing,
upper levels on essays — is widely used though increasingly considered
inadequate.

At many institutions, writing labs or centers (often with computers) support —
but usually do not replace — BW classes, with instruction from tutors
supervised by a writing specialist.

## Two controversies

Two controversies have generated spirited debate. The first concerns
eliminating BW by absorbing its students into regular first-year English
courses — strong opinions notwithstanding, few institutions seem to be
pursuing it. The second concerns a "re-vision" and criticism of the work of
the late Mina Shaughnessy, a key pioneer of the field; such views receive some
attention, but recognition of the value of Shaughnessy's work remains intact.
