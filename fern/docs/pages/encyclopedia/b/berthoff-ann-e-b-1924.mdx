Educator known for basing her rhetoric on her experience as a writing teacher
and addressing it to other educators. She holds degrees from Radcliffe and
Cornell College and taught writing for forty-five years, with assignments at
the University of Massachusetts, Vassar, Bryn Mawr, and Haverford; she has
conducted seminars or consulted for institutions as diverse as the New England
Conservatory of Music and the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratories at the University
of California, and directed the NEH seminar "Philosophy and the Composing
Process" at the University of Massachusetts/Boston.

## Rhetoric as composing, composing as thinking

Berthoff developed her rhetorical theory in response to the practical needs of
writing students. Her definition equates rhetorical activity with the act of
composing — and composing with thinking. In *Forming/Thinking/Writing*, she
asserts that rhetoric is an art of clarifying and articulating relationships as
the "rhetorician/composer" transforms information into meaning (137). One of
the primary relationships she clarifies is the dialogue between personal
conceptions and social contexts: she focuses on the interaction of the
composer's thinking with current and historical social contexts, to clarify how
the composing mind integrates those contexts as the composer initiates a
dialogue between insights and experiences.

## Against splitting cognition from feeling

Her works treat the study of rhetoric, composition, literature, science, and
philosophy as integral parts of the same study. The eclectic approach rests on
her belief that divisions between the cognitive and the affective — reason and
emotion, thinking and feeling — are a root cause of failure in rhetorical study
and application. Her answer is to insist that the rhetor, whether writer or
reader, reflect constantly on the mind's operations in order to understand how
meaning is made and thereby become a better composer. Hers is best described as
a **reflective rhetoric**: she identifies and enumerates the available means of
making a composer aware of the operations involved in the acts of reading and
writing.

## Reading the tradition

A central feature of her work is sustained reading of individual writers and
the synthesis of those readings — a short list of principal figures shows her
range across philosophy, literature, rhetoric, and language: I.A. Richards,
Susanne Langer, Charles Peirce, Edward Sapir, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, Paulo
Freire, Lev Vygotsky, and Andrew Marvell. Her work on Marvell reveals her
literary interest, and one of her tenets is that composition and literature
are best taught together.

## Legacy

Berthoff's contributions revolve around the insistence that we recognize the
purposeful activity of meaning-making as central to both private and public
discourse: significant public communication grows from rigorous private habits
of thinking, and the best ways to develop those habits are close readings of
texts and the writing of well-formed texts. Her legacy includes practical
approaches to invention and analysis using language as a heuristic, and
work in progress on the relationship between reading and composing — a
collection of essays on "language and its limits" and a study defining the
"powers of metaphor" as a way of thinking rather than metaphor as mere figure.
For Berthoff, each language act involves purposeful speculation, and all
language use begins and ends in purpose.
