<Lead>
English linguist and educator who theorized a developmental taxonomy of writing
based on the process through which children learn language. With origins in
linguistics and cognitive and developmental psychology, the taxonomy emphasizes
the expressive function of language — providing linguistic and intellective raw
material that can be developed for a variety of purposes. Applications involve
the teaching of writing, relations between writing and learning, and writing
across the curriculum.
</Lead>

## The expressive function

In *Language and Learning* (1970), Britton invokes Sapir's distinction between
the referential and expressive functions of language, emphasizing the
expressive — as opposed to the referential, with its transcription of material
reality. The expressive function makes language personal and idiosyncratic,
providing the means for individuals to connect abstract concepts with personal
experience, negotiating public and private significance and resulting in
concrete understanding and learning. Additional sources for the theory include
Vygotsky's "inner speech," Jerome Bruner's cognitive psychology with its
emphasis on the instrumental relationship between language and learning, and
Noam Chomsky's generative-transformational theory of language.

## Participant and spectator roles

In *The Development of Writing Abilities: 11–18*, an observational and
empirical analysis of school writing, Britton and his colleagues focus on the
two primary roles writers can play: the **participant role**, using language to
get things done, and the **spectator role**, using language to relive the past.
As participants, writers shape reality to an end; as spectators, writers
re-create reality.

## The three categories

Locating these roles at the ends of a continuum, Britton derives three
categories of writing:

1. **Transactional** (participant role) — language used to accomplish the
   business of the world, further divided into *informative* writing (making
   information available) and *conative* writing (moving an audience to
   action), roughly corresponding to the traditional classifications of
   exposition and persuasion. Each involves a transaction between writer and
   reader — hence the name.
2. **Poetic** (spectator role) — language used as an art medium, a verbal icon
   whose purpose is to be an object that pleases or satisfies the writer; the
   reader's response is to share that satisfaction. In traditional terms, this
   is literary discourse, language that "exists for its own sake and not as a
   means of achieving something else" (91).
3. **Expressive** — the mediating category, representing a mode rather than a
   form: its purpose is realized through creating a text rather than describing
   the text itself. Examples include "thinking out loud on paper" (89); notes
   and drafts for writers' personal use; journal writing documenting or
   exploring immediate thoughts, feelings, and moods; and personal letters.

In the expressive mode, writers shuttle between participant and spectator
roles, generating ideas and then shaping them into language that can stand on
its own. With its generative function, expressive writing plays an obvious role
in learning to write: "Thus, in developmental terms, the expressive is a kind
of matrix from which differentiated forms of mature writing are developed"
(83). And as the link between the private and personal and the public and
social — the language of association and connection — it is the language of
learning: writing-across-the-curriculum proponents such as Toby Fulwiler
propose using expressive writing systematically in class-based journals across
all disciplines as a powerful tool for improving learning.
