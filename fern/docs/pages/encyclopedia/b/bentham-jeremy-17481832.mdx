## Life and utilitarianism

Born in London, Bentham was educated at Westminster School and entered Queen's
College, Oxford, at age twelve. The son of an attorney, he studied law and was
called to the bar but never practiced. The underpinnings of the legal system
interested him deeply, and he published *A Fragment on Government* in 1776 — a
penetrating analysis of a passage from Blackstone's *Commentaries on the Laws
of England* which, though published anonymously, earned recognition from the
Earl of Shelburne and set the course for all his later writings. Through the
Earl, Bentham met his future editor and disciple Étienne Dumont, who drafted
the *Traité de législation civile et pénale* from Bentham's French notes;
published in 1802, it was translated into English as *The Theory of
Legislation* (1864) by the American Richard Hildreth.

His best-known work remains *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and
Legislation* (1789), the classic statement of utilitarianism — the greatest
happiness principle. Many of his writings remained in manuscript until the
twentieth century.

## Language: fictions and reform

When Bentham turned to the relationship between civil and penal law, he
increasingly focused on questions of definition and the connection between law
and the use of language. At the core of these observations is the notion of
**linguistic fictions**, or incomplete symbols — forms of expression that
impute concrete qualities where none exist. The concept is a linguistic
corollary of the nominalism that dominated English philosophy in his day. As
in the legal field, Bentham was in language a codifier: he classified the
seventeen properties of language that favor linguistic reform.

Bentham profoundly influenced Ogden, who simplified English into the 850-word
system called [Basic English](/b/basic-english). The key to that simplification
was the elimination of verbs — which Bentham viewed as the slippery eels of
language.

## The boundless reformer

Bentham's boundless interests, on all of which he wrote prolifically, included
constitutional theory, social welfare, prison reform, educational reform, and
the banking system. English owes him, among other terms, *maximize*,
*minimize*, *codify*, and *international*. According to his wishes, his body
was preserved, and it continues to sit on view today as an auto-icon (his term),
clad in his everyday clothes, at University College, London — of which he was a
founder.
