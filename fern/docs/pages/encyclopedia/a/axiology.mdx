<Lead>
The general study of the theory of value and evaluation — from the Greek
*axios* (value, worth) and *logos* (account, reason, theory).
</Lead>

## Origins of the term

The term was apparently first used by the French philosopher Paul Lapie in
*Logic of the Will* (1902); "Syllabus of Axiology" (*Grundriss der Axiologie*)
was the title of Part 5 of Eduard von Hartmann's *System of Philosophy in
Outline* (1908); the first English use was W.M. Urban's *Valuation* (1909). As
a major division of philosophy, axiology — dealing with "the good" — is a
companion to epistemology and metaphysics, which deal with "the true" and "the
real." It includes the more specific studies of the morally good (ethics) and
the beautiful (aesthetics). Many philosophers prefer the phrase "value
theory."

Bahm surveys four major axiological viewpoints: **Hedonism** (intrinsic value
in pleasant feelings), **Voluntarism** (intrinsic value in satisfaction of
desire), **Romanticism** (intrinsic value in desire itself, apathy as evil),
and **Anandism** (intrinsic good in feeling contented, at one with the
universe, nirvana) (57–64).

## Axiology comes to composition

Several speech-communication scholars had used the term, but it was not
introduced into composition until Fulkerson's 1990 paper "Composition Theory
in the Eighties: Axiological Consensus and Paradigmatic Diversity." Value
judgments pervade composition: from the initial decision that it is good to
read and write, to decisions about which classroom activities promote those
abilities, to the writer's choice between one introduction or one word and
another, to peer and teacher assessment. The task of an axiological
perspective is to articulate the principles by which such judgments are — or
ought to be — made.

When Fulkerson asserted that compositionists were approaching "axiological
consensus," he meant they were converging on an answer to "What constitutes
good writing?" A decade earlier the field had been divided among four
conflicting theories — the **expressive**, the **mimetic**, the **formalist**,
and the **rhetorical**. By the end of the eighties, he argued, the field had
essentially agreed that good writing is defined *contextually*: writing that
achieves the writer's goals within a rhetorical situation.

The traditional distinction between **instrumental** (means) values and
**ultimate** (ends, intrinsic) values clarifies the shift: the rhetorical
view treats good writing as instrumental — a means to effects on readers that
the writer values (persuasion or communication, broadly) — not a good in
itself. The other three textual axiologies can be read as treating the
well-written paper as an intrinsic good: for the formalist, a paper is good
because it has the desired form, irrespective of any end achieved; the same
reasoning applies *mutatis mutandis* to expressivism and mimeticism.

## Values beyond texts

There is a broader sense in which axiological theory applies to composition:
teaching writing is also necessarily teaching a way of seeing and acting in
the world. Proponents of the "axiological" approach to rhetoric maintain that
to teach discourse means teaching not just the values of texts but values
beyond texts (Eubanks 1978). Whenever one teaches discourse, one teaches a
view of what responsible intellectual life is. Extratextual value questions
addressed in a writing classroom include:

1. Which topics are worth discussing, and which trivial?
2. Do writers have a responsibility to become informed about their topics?
3. Does one have a responsibility to provide evidence or logical support for
   claims?
4. Is it acceptable to engage in fallacious, emotive, or propagandistic
   attempts to communicate?
5. What constitutes fair and accurate use of other texts?
6. Can one equally well argue for any side of any issue (for instance, pro-racism
   or pro-Hitler)?
7. Do writers have a responsibility to further social justice?

Ralph Eubanks and Virgil Baker argue that the modern world suffers from
"axiological impotence" (341) and that a major function of rhetorical
education should be to help alleviate the crisis in values and valuation:
Western rhetoric has always asked and answered key value questions — what is
preferable, what is just — and rhetoric, says Eubanks, is "applied axiology"
(215).

## The axiological paradox

Modern compositionists tend to accept as axiomatic that neither language nor a
perspective can be value-neutral: the writer is enmeshed in value issues even
before beginning to write. Garko and Cissna describe the two positions:

> The "axiological" perspective holds that values and communication are
> inherently linked. The "positivist" view implies that values and
> communication have or ought to have nothing to do with one another.

Contemporary composition is militantly antipositivist. As the postmodern
aphorism goes, we are "always already" situated; there is no view without a
preexistent viewpoint — hence the formulation that all teaching is ideological
and that teachers should at least become conscious of their own ideological
presuppositions.

But that raises a further axiological issue: are all ideologies created
equal? Is there no way to make reasoned choices among them? And is a writing
teacher justified in (1) focusing the class on the study of ideology and (2)
requiring students to agree with the teacher's viewpoint? Some teachers,
identifying as Marxists, focus their classes overtly on critiquing the
dominant ideology, committing to a "liberatory" or "radical" pedagogy; others
maintain that rhetoric courses should teach not *what* to say, only *how* to
say well what students choose. Both views make axiological commitments — but
of contrasting sorts. It is impossible to teach rhetoric without axiological
presuppositions, yet the presuppositions are themselves value judgments, and
there seems to be no way of adjudicating between contrary sets of commitments.
This is the axiological paradox.
