<Lead>
The rhetoric of advertising — the symbol systems represented in aural, visual,
and verbal advertising texts — has been a subject of analysis across several
disciplines throughout the twentieth century. This entry surveys the areas of
cultural and literary theory that inform current rhetorical perspectives on
advertising.
</Lead>

## Why advertising attracts rhetorical analysis

Current theory from a variety of language-oriented disciplines encourages
rhetorical applications to advertising — including the conscious merging of
ideological analysis and textual criticism — perhaps more than to any other
field. Several disciplines have carried out rhetorical analyses of such text:

- **Language disciplines** have been strongly influenced by literary theorists
  such as Stanley Fish and Mikhail Bakhtin, and by feminists Jane Tompkins in
  America and Julia Kristeva and Hélène Cixous in France — work that broadened
  the notion of textuality and the cultural territory texts are drawn from.
- **Speech and communication** have broadened from a traditional focus on
  political discourse to a far wider range of cultural texts and media, and
  have absorbed theorists traditionally associated with the social sciences
  and humanities.
- **Historical applications** of rhetorical theory, such as recent work on the
  cultural context of the Greek sophists, now analyze texts of "low" as well
  as "high" culture — a distinction effectively blurred, if not eliminated.
  As the range of texts has expanded, so has the focus: on complex socially
  constructed contexts rather than texts alone. The texts become windows
  opened onto new social realities.

Two emerging fields have collectively influenced all of these areas:
**composition**, which uses classical and modern rhetoric to build
theory-based pedagogies in which rhetorical analysis of culture supports
critical writing, and **cultural studies**, which since the 1960s has brought
neo-Marxist and new social theory and criticism to mass-media contexts.

## Fantasy theme theory

Ernest G. Bormann's fantasy theme theory provides an effective methodology
for analyzing advertising because it combines several elements of cultural
criticism: the social construction of truth and reality, the examination of
cultural myths, the dramatization of those myths in everyday life and
language, and the investigation of the personal feelings and values bound to
cultural myths that make persuasive communication possible.

A fantasy theme, for Bormann, is something that happened to a group in the
past or a dream of what the group might do in the future. A fantasy theme
"chains out" — becomes dramatized — developing within the group a common
culture in which values and attitudes are tested and legitimized. A
**rhetorical vision**, a composite drama constructed from chained-out fantasy
themes, contains motives that impel group members to act and to ignore
contradictions based on logic or prior knowledge.

<Example>
The perception of an unspoiled frontier — ready to be conquered, used, or
escaped into — that appears in many automobile and cigarette advertisements
is a rhetorical vision. Such advertisements populate space with symbolic
characters, actions, or gestures that complete the fantasy vision.
</Example>

Combined with ideologically sensitive criticism — feminist critique, with its
perceptions of stereotyped representation, or the constructionism of Michel
Foucault, which intensifies awareness of the hegemonic control that visual,
aural, and verbal language exerts on consumers — fantasy theme analysis
yields methods with powerful critical and pedagogical implications.

## Kenneth Burke and Mikhail Bakhtin

Two theorists, working in very different cultural contexts, show how literary
and historical methods can become powerful tools of cultural interpretation.

**Burke**, working from a modernist critical perspective on "high" culture in
the 1920s and influenced by the upheavals of the 1930s and 1940s, produced
rhetorical analyses of cultural artifacts ranging from Hitler's *Mein Kampf*
to advertising slogans and popular song lyrics. His critical scope is best
represented in his dramatistic theory — the critical apparatus he called the
**Pentad** — which applies a basic formula for human action within cultural
contexts to the social-psychological dynamic of particular texts.

**Bakhtin**, a classicist who built on studies of classical and folk
traditions in the novel, developed two seminal concepts:

1. **Heteroglossic text** — texts that bring together different linguistic
   registers, social-political frameworks, and ideologies, unified by themes
   that cross those boundaries. The critical-interpretive act learns to read
   such texts as dynamic social entities whose voices interact to reveal
   deeper social, political, and psychological processes.
2. **The carnival metaphor** — the dynamic, unpredictable ways a text's
   language interacts with the social classes and groupings of its readers.
   The interpreter cannot look for one-to-one relationships between textual
   structure and readers, because texts are heteroglossic and readers are
   constructed out of many contending, overlapping communities.

Burke's dramatism and Bakhtin's carnivalesque give the critic and teacher of
advertising rhetoric the broad base needed to go beyond surface analysis —
once past the surface, the latent complexities of advertising reveal
themselves to critic, teacher, and student, and can become a conscious part
of the meaning we take from the rhetoric of advertising.
