<Lead>
Nowhere else in the extant Roman rhetorics is the tendency to codify as
apparent as in the rhetoricians' discussions of how a speech needed to be
structured.
</Lead>

## 1. Exordium

The introduction to a persuasive address required the student to select
materials that would either enhance or make repugnant the case at hand
(*ad Herennium* 1.4; *De inventione* 1.xiv.19ff; *De oratore* 1.143). Topics
could be found by analyzing the ethos of the speaker, the character of the
opponent, the audience, and the facts of the case — the catalogs of topics
working much like a checklist, a highly systematized way of exploring every
conceivable dimension for producing a powerful, impact-filled introduction.
The function of the exordium was to render listeners attentive and well
disposed and to set the tone for the oration. A speaker in a weak position,
however, was taught indirect approaches to undermine and sabotage an
opponent's case in the introduction itself — through humor, apparent
agreement, and insinuation.

## 2. Narratio

The narrative was divided into types with attendant rules for each: a
straightforward offering of facts; a story interweaving facts through the
entire speech; or a presentation essentially unconnected with the case. The
emphasis, whichever type was selected, was always on traits of character. The
rules were unequivocal: the narrative should contain only the essentials,
should be clear, sequenced, and plausible. Even when the facts were true, one
should still make them plausible — the narrative serves as an anticipatory
reputation. When an orator builds the story so that events fit what is usual,
expected, or natural, the motives of the persons involved become quite
apparent to an audience.

## 3. Divisio

In a typical classical forensic oration, a division occurred next
(*ad Herennium* 1.x.17): a section covering agreements and disagreements and
presenting an overview of the entire case to the auditors.

## 4. Confirmatio

Gaining belief from one's audience was paramount. The proof was designed
specifically to work on existing beliefs (*ad Herennium* 2.xviii.28). Using
the [stasis](/s/stasis-theory) formula, one could determine the crux of a case
and argue from conjecture, definition, quality, or transference. As in the
narrative, rhetoricians placed great stress on motives and probabilities.
Rules covered speaking for and against witnesses, testimony, rumors, documents,
and conflicting laws; lists of topical headings for each were committed to
memory in the schools. Various commonplaces were considered proper here as
well — designed for recommending pity or attacking the prosecutor.

Roman rhetoricians more or less agreed on the structure a proof should take,
most favoring the **epicheireme**, a five-part form: begin with a proposition;
offer a reason for it; add a further proof of the reason; then provide
embellishment and a résumé. Extensive rules were supplied for each of the five
parts.

## 5. Refutatio and peroratio

The refutation formed the fifth part of the oration, its purpose the
recognition of faults and flaws in the opponent's proofs. Finally came the
[peroration](/p/peroratio), summing up the key features of the case,
amplifying the salient points via commonplaces, and adding any final appeals.
