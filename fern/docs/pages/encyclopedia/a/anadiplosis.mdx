---
title: "Anadiplosis"
subtitle: "Repeating a clause's last word as the next clause's first"
---

<Lead>
The repetition of the last word of one line or clause to begin the next — a
device that chains sentences together link by link.
</Lead>

## Emotional force

Albert Camus uses anadiplosis in *L'Étranger*: "To me, it's a tragedy. A
tragedy, everyone knows what that is. It leaves you defenseless." The device
can infuse emotional force into a passage, as in Bossuet's *Oraison funèbre
de la duchesse d'Orléans*:

> Both princes and population groaned in vain; in vain did the King's
> brother, in vain did the King himself clasp Madame to his bosom.

## Dialogue and argument

In dialogue, anadiplosis serves to link replies together, as in this exchange
from *King Lear*:

> Cornwall: Fetch forth the stocks! As I have life and honor, there shall he
> sit till noon.
> Regan: Till noon? Till night, my lord, and all night too!

Anadiplosis can also lay out the premises of a syllogism, as Richard III does
in exclaiming:

> Come! I have learn'd that fearful commenting / Is leaden servitor to dull
> delay; / Delay leads impotent and snail-pac'd beggary. / Then fiery
> expedition be my wing. (*Richard III*, 4.3.51)

## Chains and the gradatio

When used to string together a series of clauses, anadiplosis can produce
either a catalogue or a [Gradatio](/g/gradatio). Paul demonstrates the chain
form in writing:

> More than that, we rejoice in our suffering, knowing that suffering
> produces endurance, endurance produces character, and character produces
> hope, and hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured
> into our hearts through the Holy Spirit which has been given to us.
> (Romans 5.3–5)
