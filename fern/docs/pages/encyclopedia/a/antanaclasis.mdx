<Lead>
From the Greek "reflection, bending back": the repetition of the same word in
two contrasting senses to create a pun.
</Lead>
<Badge intent="note">Figure of Speech</Badge>


## Puns and aphorisms

> Your argument is sound, nothing but sound. (Benjamin Franklin)

> The long cigarette that's long on flavor. (Pall Mall advertisement)

Antanaclasis is frequent in aphorisms:

> O mortal man, think mortal thoughts! (Euripides)

> True eloquence takes no heed of eloquence, true morality takes no heed of
> morality. (Pascal)

## Taking over an opponent's words

The device often appears in dialogue, where a speaker takes up the other's
words and changes their meaning to their own advantage. Shakespeare uses it
this way when Armado declares, "By the North Pole, I challenge thee," and
Costard replies:

> I will not fight with a pole, like a Northern man. I'll slash; I'll do it
> by the sword. (*Love's Labor's Lost*, 5.2.699)
