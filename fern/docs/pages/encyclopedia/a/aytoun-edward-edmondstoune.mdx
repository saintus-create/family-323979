Scottish poet, humorist, and university reformer who held Edinburgh's chair of
rhetoric and belles lettres from 1845 until his death in 1865 — and used it to
make English literature a university subject in Scotland a generation before

## A chair with an identity problem

The belles-lettres tradition was firmly established at Edinburgh when Aytoun
took the chair in 1845, yet he had little patience for the discipline's
classical framing. In his lectures he dismissed the art of rhetoric as
worthless in itself, attacked the "artificiality" of a system buried under
rules, and warned that following them all would reduce speech to
"a tissue of woven sophistry." His preference was clear: in the introductory
lecture he said he would far rather occupy a chair of English language and
literature than of rhetoric and belles lettres. The surviving manuscript shows
that sentence revised — from "I would much rather" to "I am delighted to sit
in this chair" — once his actual title caught up with his ambitions.

## A popular and consequential teacher

Aytoun's lectures were a phenomenon: Edinburgh townspeople turned out alongside
students to hear him on English literature. Enrollment in the course rose from
twenty-seven to 150 over his tenure, and his standing helped persuade the Royal
Commission of 1861 to recommend adding English literature to the curriculum of
every Scottish university.

## Widening access, renaming the field

Aytoun defended the Scottish openness to students who arrived with
"rudimentary" preparation and opposed entrance examinations that shut out
poorer Scots. Student fees paid professors' salaries, so the chair's off-putting
title was no small matter: in the popular mind, "Rhetoric" read as a trivial
art and "Belles Lettres" as aimless criticism. He proposed renaming the course
"English Literature and Composition." The Royal Commissioners of 1858–1862 met
him partway — they doubled his salary and renamed the post the Regius
Professorship of Rhetoric and English Literature.

## Papers and reputation

The National Library of Scotland holds a substantial Aytoun collection
(manuscripts 4897–4928: lectures, accounts, correspondence). The rhetoric
lectures are conventional surveys of the traditional system, threaded with
running criticism of it; the introductory lecture, laying out his thinking on
education and the study of English literature, is the most interesting document
in the set. Today Aytoun is remembered in Scotland chiefly for his ballad
collections — an afterlife he shares with his American contemporary Francis
Child, Harvard's pioneer professor of English literature.
