<Lead>
A figure of speech that strings together words, phrases, or clauses saying
essentially the same thing, so that a point lands not once but with
accumulating weight.
</Lead>
<Badge intent="note">Figure of Speech</Badge>


## What the repetition accomplishes

Classical and Renaissance rhetoricians identify two distinct effects. The
first is **amplification**: repeating near-synonyms inflates the emotional
surface of a claim. The *Rhetorica ad Herennium* offers the canonical example —
a prosecution that stacks accusation on accusation:

> He is the betrayer of his own self-respect, and the waylayer of the
> self-respect of others; covetous, intemperate, irascible, arrogant; disloyal
> to his parents, ungrateful to his friends. (I.xl)

The second is **familiarization**: dwelling on an idea makes an audience
comfortable with it. Shakespeare plays the effect for laughs in *Henry IV,
Part 2*, where Shallow refuses Falstaff's plea with one restatement after
another:

> I will not excuse you; you shall not be excus'd; excuses shall not be
> admitted; there is no excuse shall serve you; you shall not be excus'd.
> (5.1.5)

## How it differs from antithesis

Accumulatio is open-ended — the number of restatements is arbitrary and can
keep growing. Its counterpart [Antithesis](/a/antithesis) closes on exactly two
balanced terms. Where you want a judgment to feel exhaustive, pile up; where
you want it to feel complete and final, set two ideas against each other.
