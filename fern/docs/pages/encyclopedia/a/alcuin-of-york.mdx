## From York to Aachen

Alcuin was educated at York, a leading center of learning for both
ecclesiastical and classical studies, and spent most of his life there. He
began teaching at the cathedral school in 768 and was made headmaster ten
years later. A bibliophile, he acquired many Christian and secular works —
especially Virgil — occasionally traveling to the continent to buy books or
meet other scholars. On one of those sojourns, in 781, he met Charlemagne,
who offered him the headship of the palace school.

Charlemagne apparently recognized in Alcuin the kind of teacher and scholar
he admired, believing the Frankish court could become a major center of
learning under his direction. During Alcuin's fourteen years in the position
(782–796), Aachen attracted visitors from all over Europe; notable scholars
and dignitaries, as well as Charlemagne himself and his family, became his
students. The curriculum grew from the liberal arts tradition: the
[trivium](/t/trivium) — grammar, rhetoric, dialectic — and the
[quadrivium](/q/quadrivium) — music, astronomy, arithmetic, and geometry. But
the overall program was decidedly ecclesiastical: among Alcuin's most
significant scholarly efforts were revisions of the Vulgate and the Gregorian
sacramentary, and he represented Charlemagne's iconoclastic views at the
Council of Frankfurt in 794.

## The Disputatio de rhetorica

Alcuin's chief contribution to the rhetorical tradition was his textbook on
rhetoric and the virtues (794), a fictionalized dialogue between Charlemagne
and Alcuin. The work is primarily a compendium of Ciceronian doctrine —
especially Cicero's *De inventione* and Julius Victor's *Ars rhetorica* —
labeled by Howell "a work of popularization" (33). What is original is what
Alcuin supplied or adapted for ecclesiastical purposes, such as applying
epideictic terms to the homily. Most important, he changed the purpose of the
four virtues — prudence, justice, courage, and temperance — from the
Ciceronian use as topics for invention to moral themes or goals in public
questions for the responsible sovereign and his subjects (Howell 63–64).

## Tours and legacy

In reward for his service, Alcuin was given charge of the monks at
Saint-Martin-de-Tours in 796. There he continued to teach and more frequently
to write for himself, composing numerous letters of moral counsel to bishops,
kings, and even Pope Leo III. When he died on May 19, 804, he had good reason
to believe he had served his age well — if not greatly advancing learning,
then certainly protecting it.
