<Lead>
A treatise written c. 353 B.C.E. by Isocrates on rhetorical and educational
theory and on the formation of the public man. Composed long after *Against
the Sophists*, the *Antidosis* offers the clearest glimpse of Isocrates'
theories of education and rhetoric.

## The civilizing power of logos

Eminent among Isocrates' discussions, according to Bizzell and Herzberg, is
"a long panegyric on the civilizing powers of the logos" (45). The *logos*,
for Isocrates, is exercised through interdisciplinary training across the
trivium and rhetorical studies — though with a practical caution:

> I hold that men who wish to do some good in the world must banish utterly
> from their interests all vain speculations and all activities which have no
> bearing on our lives. (51)

Trained and used properly — "by the best of conventional morality" — the
*logos* becomes the foundation of successful oratorical training and of the
effective orator (45). It also favorably influences the other aspects of
persuasion: "the power to speak well and think right will reward the man who
approaches the art of discourse with a love of wisdom and love of honor"
(52). These dual "loves" lead the orator to study and use "examples which
are the most illustrious and the most edifying," and, habituating himself to
contemplate and appraise such examples, "he will feel their influence not
only in the preparation of a given discourse, but in all actions in his
life" (52).

## The public intellectual

These theories anticipate the not-yet-formed Roman notions of rhetoric and of
the orator's training. Susan Jarratt notes that Isocrates, among other
sophists, took great care in developing a "model of the public intellectual"
(98) — individuals formed by contact with a multitude of disciplines who did
"not allow their minds to be dried up by these barren subtleties, nor to be
stranded on the speculations of the ancient sophists" (51). The public
intellectual, for Isocrates, is public in a double sense: formed by
information in the public domain, and using that information to serve and
advance society.

## Models, emulation, persona

The connection between education and rhetorical theory that Isocrates
espouses can thus be put to work: study good models, emulate them in action,
and use them in discourse. The rhetor thereby constructs an eminent public
persona — one that will "establish a most honorable name among his fellow
citizens" (52).
