title: "Anastrophe"
subtitle: "Turning the usual order of words upside down"
---

<Lead>
From the Greek "to turn upside down": the reversal of the usual order of
words, as opposed to the displacement of a single element.
</Lead>
<Badge intent="note">Figure of Speech</Badge>

## Reversal as emphasis

Where [Hyperbaton](/h/hyperbaton) emphasizes the displacement of a single
element, anastrophe emphasizes the reversal of two:

> In my years lusty, many a deed doughty did I. (Puttenham)

> Figures pedantical. (Shakespeare, *Love's Labor's Lost*, 5.2.407)

More typically, anastrophe is used to draw attention to — and emphasize —
the words that have been reversed:

> Are you good men and true? (*Much Ado About Nothing*, 3.3.1)

## Unstringing conventional meaning

Sometimes the device works against ordinary sense rather than for emphasis,
as in Eliot's "Burnt Norton" (*Four Quartets*):

> Time present and time past / Are both perhaps present in time future, /
> And time future contained in time past.

Verbs can be reversed with their related nouns or adjectives as easily as
nouns with adjectives. Cervantes reverses verb and predicate adjective in:

> For if he like a madman lived, / At least he like a wise one died.
