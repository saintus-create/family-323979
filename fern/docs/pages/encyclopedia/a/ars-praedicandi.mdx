<Lead>
Formalized guidelines for preparing and delivering sermons that have served
ministers of mainline churches throughout much of the history of
Christianity. The *ars praedicandi* comes down through history as published
handbooks or lectures; today published as book-length preaching manuals not
unlike college composition textbooks, the art of preaching is one of the most
vital and long-lived rhetorical genres in North America.
</Lead>
<Badge intent="tip">Classical Work</Badge>

## A classically influenced genre

Throughout its history, the writers of preaching manuals have been influenced
by — or involved in writing — secular rhetorics. Beginning with St.
Augustine's *On Christian Doctrine*, preaching manuals have absorbed the
secular rhetorical theories of their age. Augustine adapted Ciceronian
rhetoric to the task of preaching, reversing the early church's trend against
secular rhetoric:

> While the faculty of eloquence, which is of great value in urging either
> evil or justice, is, in itself indifferent, why should it not be obtained
> for the uses of the good in the service of truth . . . ?

Augustine — and, notably, Martin Luther — urged preachers to study rhetoric in
school. Many rhetoricians thereafter wrote both secular and sacred rhetorics,
including François Fénelon, George Campbell, and Hugh Blair, and most
preaching manuals up to the early twentieth century show the significant
influence of classical and Enlightenment theory. Whatever else may be said of
it, the art of preaching has been an academic, classically influenced
rhetorical genre throughout its history.

## Arrangement and style

Until the nineteenth century, the dominant elements of preaching manuals were
considerations of arrangement and style. Style classifications are the main
element Augustine borrows from Cicero; associating particular sacred purposes
with particular styles (the plain style for instruction, for example) is an
enduring feature of the art. Some twentieth-century manuals are organized
around a single style — narrative preaching, an extension of Cicero's plain
style.

On arrangement, authors of preaching manuals have argued that a sermon's
structure is critical to its effectiveness. Medieval manuals, which introduced
"thematic preaching," advised on the divisions and subdivisions of sermons —
influencing a style Roger Bacon dismissed as "endless divisions and
quibblings, in which there is neither sublimity of style nor depth of wisdom."
In the eighteenth century, arrangement was simplified to two parts — explication
and application — a trend that, along with the use of psychology in sermon
development, endures in many modern manuals. In the late twentieth century the
art began to shed its ethnocentrism, with new manuals and scholarship focusing
on feminist sermons, the black preaching tradition, and Jewish preaching
practices.
