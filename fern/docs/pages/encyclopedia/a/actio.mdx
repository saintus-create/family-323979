<Lead>
*Actio*, Latin for "action" or "doing," names the effective use of physical
gesture and vocal delivery when presenting a speech. It is the Latin term for
the effective delivery of a speech.
</Lead>
<Badge intent="info">Canon of Rhetoric</Badge>

## A fifth part of oratory

Quintilian writes in Book 3 of the *Institutio Oratoria* that *actio* is
synonymous with *pronuntiatio*, or delivery — the fifth part of the art of
oratory. The first four are *inventio* (invention or discovery),
*dispositio* (arrangement or organization), *elocutio* (style), and
*memoria* (memorizing the speech). Later in the *Institutio Oratoria*,
however, Quintilian — citing Cicero — distinguishes between the two terms:
*actio* referring to physical gesture, *pronuntiatio* to the voice. Whichever
term one uses, he concludes, delivery has a "powerful effect in oratory"
(Book 11).

## Classical endorsements

Before Quintilian, both Aristotle and Cicero had stressed the importance of
delivering speeches effectively. In Book 3 of the *Rhetoric*, Aristotle
considers delivery "of the greatest importance." In *De oratore*, Cicero
asserts that delivery "is the dominant factor in oratory," repeating the story
that Demosthenes, asked what mattered most in oratory, ranked delivery first,
second, and third (Book 3).

## The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Eighteenth-century rhetoricians continued to assign *actio* an important
place. Hugh Blair (1718–1800) devotes Lecture 33 of the *Lectures on Rhetoric
and Belles Lettres* (1783) to delivery, covering loudness of voice,
distinctness of articulation, and the slowness and appropriateness of
pronunciation, along with emphases, pauses, tones, and gestures
(11.206–10). Earlier in the nineteenth century, Richard Whately
(1787–1863) considered the subject important enough to devote all of Part 4
of the *Elements of Rhetoric* (1828) to it, under the heading "Of Elocution,
or Delivery."

Later in the century, however, the growing split between spoken and written
rhetoric became apparent. Alexander Bain (1818–1903) gives the topic less
than one page of the 343-page *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866),
noting only that the "Demeanor of the Speaker includes certain points
affecting the orator's success" — by which he means the speaker's "tone and
manner in general" (256).

## Decline — and a possible revival

In the twentieth century, as speech departments and English departments
divided in most American schools and colleges, *actio* and the delivery of
speeches received comparatively little attention. The turn of the century,
though, may see renewed interest as a result of the revived emphasis on the
"whole language" approach — the integration of reading, writing, speaking,
and listening. The English Coalition Conference of 1987, convened under the
aegis of the National Council of Teachers of English, passed resolutions
including one that reads, in part: "The ability to communicate their
[students'] views in oral and written form . . . is also indispensable to
citizens in a democracy."
