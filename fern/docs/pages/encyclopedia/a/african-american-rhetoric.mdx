serve as complex responses to the oppression they experienced as slaves and

## The oral foundation

Often legally prohibited from learning to read and write, enslaved people
developed robust oral traditions, passing down spirituals, sermons, and
folktales from one generation to the next. Without this cultural and
rhetorical process, African-Americans as a group might not have survived at
all; with it, they produced orators such as Sojourner Truth, whose illiteracy
did not interfere with her eloquence or leadership.

The oral culture has persisted through the twentieth century. Martin Luther
King, Jr., was nurtured by a folk pulpit tradition developed by slaves and
continued by others — including King's father, who began sermonizing when he
could barely read and write. In King's final speech, "I've Been to the
Mountaintop," he adapted the slaves' religious world view, writing a garbage
workers' strike into the biblical story of the Exodus. Jesse Jackson
delivered a variation of a sermon dating to at least the 1860s. The gospel
music of Thomas Dorsey, Mahalia Jackson, and James Cleveland sprang from the
folk music of spirituals and blues, and in some respects rap music derives
from and resembles folk sermons. "We Shall Overcome" is a showcase of folk
composition: a hymn around the turn of the century, a Charleston labor song
in the 1940s, and by the 1960s a national and international civil rights
anthem.

## The slave narrative and the autobiographical tradition

Related to the oral traditions is autobiography — the most significant genre
of African-American writing. Scores of slave narratives (sometimes transcribed
and framed by whites) sold in extremely large numbers, persuading thousands
of Northerners of the brutality and hypocrisy of slavery. Frederick Douglass
wrote three of the most thoughtful, masterfully transmuting his personal
traumas into a call for radical social change; Harriet Jacobs explained her
owner's sexual advances and the travails of her escape. Personal experience
serves as a powerful vehicle for political protest.

The tradition persists: almost every important African-American literary and
political figure — from W.E.B. Du Bois and Booker T. Washington to James
Baldwin and JoAnn Robinson — has written autobiographically, intertwining
personal revelation with analysis of racial exploitation, accommodation, and
resistance. Two of the most brilliant, *All God's Dangers* by Nate Shaw and
*The Autobiography of Malcolm X*, are as-told-to autobiographies recorded and
transcribed by others.

## Women's self-definition and recovery

African-American women writers such as Anne Moody, Maya Angelou, Audre Lorde,
and bell hooks use autobiographical forms to define themselves and — in
hooks's words — to "talk back," challenging established rules of speech and
conduct for black women. Mapping the terrain where the private and the social
intersect, these writers insist that only there is radical change possible.

Editors such as Gerda Lerner and Henry Louis Gates, Jr., are compiling
documentary histories, journals, and writings by and about African-American
women, long neglected or misrepresented. Drawing on suppressed records and
oral testimony (including court proceedings), these works chronicle public
figures such as Harriet Tubman and Sojourner Truth, but many record
little-known women whose stories reveal a pattern of survival and resistance.

Zora Neale Hurston, drawing on oral testimony she collected as a folklorist,
made her 1937 novel *Their Eyes Were Watching God* showcase a female
protagonist who sheds the conventional straitjackets of race and sex and
reenvisiones herself and her community. Contemporary writers such as Toni
Morrison and Alice Walker (who resurrected Hurston's work) likewise rewrite
oral testimony and diaries — "in search," in Walker's words, of their
"mothers' gardens." For them, as for Douglass, Jacobs, Du Bois, Hurston, and
Malcolm X before them, self-definition becomes a prerequisite to social
change. Like their predecessors, King, Walker, Morrison, and Angelou have
become role models and mentors for African-Americans who want to reenvision
their lives, tell their stories, and transform the world.
