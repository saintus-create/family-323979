<Lead>
In rhetoric, the repetition of the same word or phrase at the start of
successive clauses (Greek *ana-*, "up," plus *pherein*, "to carry"). In modern
linguistics the same word names something broader: any expression that takes
its meaning from an earlier expression in the discourse.
</Lead>

<Info>
One term, two research programs. Read the sections below in order — most
confusion about "anaphora" comes from mixing the rhetorical figure with the
linguistic mechanism.
</Info>

## The rhetorical figure

Traditional rhetoric restricts anaphora to repetition in clause-initial
position. Classical analysts credited the device with force and emphasis in
Greek oratory, and the analytical habit stuck: it surfaced in the
interpretation of biblical texts, then in the stylistic study of literary
authors. Critics have heard Dickens's anaphora as a taste for tidiness, Joyce
extending it to whole sentences to carry a stream of consciousness, and Orwell
pushing it to the point of political menace. Critical discourse analysis keeps
the tradition alive by reading anaphora in real texts as a way power and
dominance get enacted in language.

## The linguistic sense

Discourse analysis and pragmatics use *anaphora* for any linguistic element
whose referent was established earlier — in the same clause, a neighboring
one, or pages back. The tie can take many forms:

- **Exact repetition** — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God."
- **Synonym or paraphrase** — *Mary eloped with Bill. The woman is a fool.*
- **Pronoun** — *All candidates think they can win.*
- **Demonstrative** — *Mary was concerned about her own welfare, as well as that of others.*
- **Epithet** — *I invited John to dinner, but I can't stand the idiot.*
- **Verb-phrase ellipsis** — *Bill accused Tom of stealing his wallet, but Tom didn't do it.*
- **Silence** — even a linguistic "zero" can point backward: *John wants ∅ [John] to go.*
