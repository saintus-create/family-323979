<Lead>
Three related senses: (1) a **process** of controversy, dispute, inference, and
critique; (2) a dialectical or dialogical **procedure** used in discussion and
debate; and (3) a logical **product** — a serial predication, an utterance, a
textual object open to analysis and critique; a linguistically explicable claim
supported by a reason.
</Lead>

## From quarrel to discipline

In popular parlance, "argument" suggests a disagreeable confrontation —
American journalists use it interchangeably with *altercation*, *quarrel*,
even *fight*. In academic parlance the connotations are more agreeable:

- **Social scientists** study argument as a valuable empirical occurrence in
  thought, conversation, small groups, organizations, legal proceedings, and
  mass communication.
- **Humanists** treat argumentation as a pedagogically valuable discipline and
  study arguments as instances of rational discourse.
- **Rhetorical scholars** use "argument" for a variety of processes: creative
  invention, public reason-giving and influence, symbolic enactment, and the
  joint creation of situations and identities.
- **Logicians** construe arguments as serial predications — linguistically
  explicable sets of premises and conclusions.

Joseph W. Wenzel's rubrics — **process, procedure, product** — were
introduced to distinguish the critical norms derived from rhetoric, dialectic,
and logic, and they serve to cluster these scholarly interests.

## Argument as process

Process-oriented researchers take naturally occurring talk as primary data and
see argument as a phenomenon that flares up and simmers down in social
interaction. Interactions become arguments when overt opposition is present.
The main empirical question is why the process is coherent: how do arguers
know they are arguing, and why are their claims and counterclaims mutually
intelligible as claims and counterclaims?

One answer (Willard 1987): opposition *per se* is a kind of relationship or
co-orientation in which interactants, taking one another's perspectives, see
themselves as engaged in a reciprocal process of claims and counterclaims.
Naive social actors impute argumentative intentions to one another and frame
their behavior accordingly (Benoit). Another answer (Barbara J. O'Keefe and
Pamela Benoit): argumentative competence is a special case of general
knowledge about language, conversational structure, and interaction — even
very young children know how to assert, request, promise, take turns, and
respond appropriately. "In producing arguments children call on their general
conversational skills" (173); their arguments are processes of managing or
resolving opposition governed by general principles of discourse coherence.

**Conversation analysis** has studied argument-for-coherence most extensively,
with its most detailed treatment in the argumentation field in the work of
Scott Jacobs and Sally Jackson. Conversation is a process of "coordinating
plans and negotiating meanings" (164–65) — goal-directed problem-solving
according to principles of cooperative action and practical reasoning. At the
conventional level, conversation rests on a tacit rule system that prefers
agreement: questions imply answers, requests imply granting. Disagreements
arise when preferred responses are withheld; arguments, at this simplest
level, are processes of **conversational repair**.

Conversations are not simply linear progressions of adjacency pairs
(question–answer, offer–accept, request–grant): many coherent replies are not
literal answers, and coherence does not come exclusively from the felicity
conditions of speech acts. Jackson and Jacobs report "empirical anomalies" for
a purely conventional account — people do not decipher utterances by labeling
speech-act types; they respond to perceived goals and plans. The working
presumption that others' behavior is strategic and goal-directed lets
conversationalists make sense of one another's actions. Arguments emerge as
conversationalists project, avoid, produce, or resolve disagreement —
communicative acts organized by mutual perceptions of clashing intentions.

## Argument as procedure

Approaches to argument-as-procedure are **normatively** focused: less concerned
with explaining coherence than with identifying optimal ways disagreements are
or might be disciplined. "Discipline" suggests three things:

1. **Argument is a public process.** Toulmin, Rieke, and Janik use *reasoning*
   for an interpersonal and social process of thinking and speaking, a way of
   putting ideas to the test of public standards — focusing on how claims are
   supported by reasons, how reasons are justified and opened to criticism,
   and on the principles by which some arguments are better than others.
2. **Arguments are self-regulating.** Quite apart from formal rules,
   context-specific standards of adequacy and fairness emerge as arguments
   unfold. Opponents oblige arguers to frame their behavior around the
   expectation of critique. Douglas Ehninger calls this emergent reciprocity
   **argument as method**: the method lies in opposition itself — each arguer's
   awareness of engagement with another person in attack and defense, producing
   a relationship of circumstantially equal power with mutual obligations and
   opportunities.
3. **Arguments are sometimes governed by explicit rules.** Debates formalize
   speaking time, order, and roles, and require rebuttals; parliamentary
   procedure elaborates the formalities further. The most elaborate apparatus
   is the code of conduct of the **Pragma-Dialectical** theory of the Amsterdam
   School (van Eemeren and Grootendorst 1984, 1992), meant for arguers who
   jointly intend to resolve a dispute reasonably. Arguers must agree on a
   common definition of their disagreement, on governing rules, and on
   adjudication standards. Argumentation becomes a series of complex speech
   acts by which opinions are externalized, tested against one another, and
   submitted to arbitration by a rational judge. Incorrect moves are
   **fallacies** — any speech acts that hinder the resolution of disputes.

## Arguments₁ and arguments₂

Daniel J. O'Keefe distinguishes **arguments₂** — interactions marked by
extended, overt disagreement, things people *engage in* — from
**arguments₁** — things people *make and utter*: complexes of claims and
reasons, discursive units of proof. The distinction arose from controversy
over whether arguments can be "things" at all (Brockriede), or whether any
communication in an argument₂ may be germane to explaining it (Willard).
O'Keefe defends the distinction with a **paradigm case definition**: the
clearest cases of arguments₁ "involve a linguistically explicable claim and
one or more linguistically explicable reasons" (17). Such arguments are
textual objects, found in or replicable in written documents, open to
criticism and to assessment of their warrants (the premises from which one
reasons) and the validity of their conclusions.

The paradigm case is the field's most-quoted definition, but it has not
settled how to explicate arguments₁. Borderline cases arise because texts are
open to indefinite interpretation, and because the analyst's conjectures about
nonverbal cues, relationships, and context may themselves be objected to by
the participants. Barbara O'Keefe and Pamela Benoit propose that this
fuzziness is itself a fact to be explained: "attending solely to paradigm
cases can blind the analyst to the operation of multiple attributes and
processes" (161).

## The syllogism, the enthymeme, and the Toulmin model

The debate over arguments₁ reflects a long-standing tension between logic and
persuasion: the degree to which logic is independent of the human frailties
that drive persuasion may be the degree to which it is irrelevant to human
discourse. The tension originates in Plato's distinction between perfection
and imperfection, but it is sharpest in Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, where logical
form is tied to human belief, feeling, and context. Rhetorical argument
proceeds by **induction** (examples) or **deduction** — and rhetorical
deductions are **enthymemes**, a term many translators believe meant "in the
mind."

Aristotle calls the enthymeme a rhetorical syllogism, subordinating rhetorical
proof to his larger distinction between form and matter: the enthymeme takes
the *form* of the deductive syllogism while its *matter* is the ordinary
opinions of audiences. The classic (non-Aristotelian) illustration — All
humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore Socrates is mortal — shows a
general statement, a specific case fitted into it, and an inevitable
conclusion. The rhetorical syllogism differs from the demonstrative scientific
syllogism in three respects:

1. Its premises are **probabilities**, not certainties.
2. Rhetoric is **cooperative**: persuasion cannot occur unless the listener
   participates, so premises must be probabilities the audience believes.
3. Premises **need not be fully stated** — the orator should not waste time
   saying what is obvious; listeners will supply the missing parts.

Lloyd F. Bitzer's portrait is widely accepted: rhetorical proof is a joint
creation of speaker and listener; persuaders ask premises from audiences. But
the consensus view of argumentative form is Wayne Brockriede's: the syllogism
is a **closed system** involving no inferential leaps — nothing appears in the
conclusion that was not in the premises. Since rhetorical proof is open to
doubt, uncertainty, dispute, and multiple interpretation, the criteria for a
properly formed syllogism are irrelevant to judging properly formed arguments.

The most familiar alternative is **Stephen Toulmin's functional model**, which
shifts emphasis from form to the functions statements serve in reasoning. In
Toulmin's example: that Harry was born in Bermuda is the **data** ("What do
you have to go on?"); that Harry is a British citizen is the **claim**; the
movement from data to claim is licensed by a **warrant** ("How do you get
there?") — everyone born in Bermuda is a British citizen; the **backing** for
this inference license might be the British Nationality Acts; the
**reservation** — Harry's parents were aliens, or he has become a naturalized
citizen elsewhere; and the **qualifier**: *unless* those conditions hold,
Harry is *presumably* a British subject.

The layout is a widely accepted pedagogical device, but critics question
whether naturally occurring discourse can be faithfully reduced to linear,
depersonalized, decontextualized form (Gilbert 1993); others argue the layout
is not genuinely different from the syllogism, lacks a principled basis for
distinguishing data and warrants, and can produce an unmanageable filigree of
crisscrossing lines when complex arguments are laid out (Hample 1977).

David Zarefsky points out that many disputes about arguments₁ stem from the
analysts' points of view: some study invention, some the translation of
intentions into performance, some messages, some the reasoning of listeners.
Messages, for instance, may be seen as impersonal texts, as records of
situated action, or as evidence of a speaker's beliefs about communication
(B. O'Keefe 1988) — and each view assigns the work of interpretation
differently (to the analyst, to the participants, or to a three-way
distinction among expressive, conventional, and rhetorical messages). Analysts
such as Dale Hample, who hold that arguments exist only in the cognitive
processes of arguers, will plot very different empirical pictures from those
who focus on message texts.

## Rationality: from Aristotle to holism

The idea of rationality is the field's most enduring concern, and its scope
has expanded with developments in psychology and the social grounds of
knowledge.

**Aristotle** held reason to be the most distinctively human attribute — not
an absence of emotion but a virtue that governs and keeps in proportion the
lower appetites and passions. His dialectic blended process (question and
answer, argument and counterargument) with procedure (the disputants' virtues,
civility, and respect for truth): a disciplined form of social interaction.

**Enlightenment** rhetorical theories, following the faculty psychology then
in vogue, divided the mind into discrete functions and depicted reason and
emotion as hostile dichotomies — stipulating a difference in kind between
beliefs arising from logical reason (**conviction**) and beliefs excited by
emotional appeals (**persuasion**). This dualism shaped the nineteenth
century: the sociology of knowledge described social life as so irrational
that science had to be nonsocial; argumentation arose as an academic
discipline by laying claim to the conviction side, its earliest theorists
seeing rationality as a pristine state achieved by strictly defined rules — a
pedagogy of rules keeping thought and speech uncontaminated by emotion.

The strong dualism weakened with the faculty psychology behind it. By 1917
Mary Yost argued that psychologists were abandoning dualism for holistic views
of mind: logic and emotion are interdependent cognitive processes, and there
is no such thing — in the human mind — as pristine logic. The textbooks
hedged, assigning argumentation the "primarily rational" part of rhetoric
(Winans and Utterback 1930).

The new holism did not make logic and emotion scientifically
indistinguishable. Modern attitude theory describes affective and cognitive
elements as open to conscious balancing; other theories still treat emotions
as contaminants of logic — wishful thinking distorting probability, the drive
for cognitive consistency leading people to call invalid syllogisms valid when
they agree with the conclusions.

Today many theorists defend the conviction–persuasion duality not as a
psychological fact but as a **normative goal** — reviving Aristotle's vision
of rationality as a virtue-governed balance of reason and passion. Informal
logicians such as J. Anthony Blair and Ralph Johnson use fallacy theory
pedagogically, guarding discourse from wishful thinking, suppression of
evidence, bias, *ad hominem* attack, and emotional appeal. Toulmin, Rieke,
and Janik instead see rationality as the manner in which an advocate "handles
and responds to the offering of reasons for and against claims" — one's
"openness" to argument, whose antonyms are dogmatism, close-mindedness,
zealousness, and unwillingness to risk one's ideas in the public marketplace.

## Knowledge, fields, and the public sphere

The earliest philosophies took what modern scholars call **foundationalism**:
justification is the finding or presenting of grounds, and knowledge claims
stand in systems — Aristotle and Kant assumed knowledge directly reflects the
orderly, determinate nature of reality, arranging all departments of knowledge
in an authoritative hierarchy. Rationality meant letting the system do its
work; in the least scientific domains, arguments were best grounded in the
practical disciplines — the political orator must know statecraft, diplomacy,
law, and economics.

Despite the demise of systematic philosophy, grounded knowledge remains
central: in many definitions above, the act of warranting is virtually
definitive of arguing. **Epistemic theories** of argument emphasize the social
grounds of knowledge — speech and writing take meaning and coherence from
human practices constituted in social worlds, analyzed as groups,
disciplines, professions, rhetorical communities, knowledge systems, or — in
Michel Foucault's terms — discursive formations. These units differ in their
determinism, but share the view that the grounding of claims can be analyzed
by their fit within particular judgmental systems. A common term for such
local systems is **argument fields**.

The idea originates in Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations*, which
warned against finding an expression's meaning "by contemplating the
expression itself" (601): knowledge and language divide into manifold forms of
life or language games, within which expressions take meaning from use.
Whether Wittgenstein intended epistemological relativism is contested — likely
not the extreme of unintelligibility, nor strict incomparability, since the
builder, electrician, and plumber coordinate their activities. But
proposition 12e — "If you do not keep the multiplicity of language games in
view, you will perhaps be inclined to ask questions like: 'What is a
question?'" — fundamentally limits generalization across forms of life. In
social theory, this fed the standoff between **absolutism** (a single,
universal knowledge) and **relativism**, with Jürgen Habermas's "force of the
better argument" as an organizing problem: are the grounds for appraising
claims universal, or strictly local?

Charting a middle course, Wittgenstein's student Stephen Toulmin coined
**field of argument** in *The Uses of Argument*: some standards of judgment
are field-dependent, while some standards of validity are field-invariant. In
*Human Understanding*, Toulmin divides knowledge into **rational enterprises**
arrayed along a hierarchy of compactness. Compact disciplines (atomic physics)
command substantial agreement about what counts as knowledge, their standards
subjected to continuing reflective scrutiny, their claims proportionately
trustworthy. Most fields are **diffuse** — competing schools lacking common
judgmental standards: Freudians and behaviorists each see their paradigm as
defining psychology, yet speak of different phenomena (id, ego, superego
versus conditioned responses and reinforcement) with different methods —
provincial discourses whose claims may be controversial or incomprehensible
from other perspectives.

A second reaction concerns the state of public discourse. G. Thomas Goodnight
argues the **public sphere** has been "steadily eroded by the elevation of the
personal and technical groundings of argument" (223): in the personal sphere
people are not citizens; expert authority has disenfranchised the public by
depriving it of the general knowledge needed to govern a democracy. On this
reasoning, the rationale for argumentation and critical-thinking pedagogies is
mass enfranchisement. A different approach (Willard 1987): experts,
decision-makers, and citizens share a common predicament — no individual can
grasp all subdivisions of knowledge, so everyone is authority-dependent.
Public decision-makers must adjudicate among experts, often reducing
trustworthiness to prestige and interpreting testimony through slogans and
political agendas; the onus for improving public argument lies on the expert
fields, and the discipline can serve by studying discourse across field
boundaries — how ideas are imported and exported, and how opinion changes in
border fields move inward, sometimes sparking evolutionary or revolutionary
development.

## The state of the question

Historically, these social and institutional approaches evolved from the
field's earliest focus on the solitary thinker, developing from the view that
arguments-as-products are addressed to particular audiences in concrete
situations and are valid insofar as they spark audience adherence (Perelman
and Olbrechts-Tyteca). Rationality becomes pragmatic justification (McKerrow),
and the critical question is whether judgmental conventions are local or
universal — field-dependent or field-invariant — or how particular audiences
compare to idealized abstractions such as the **universal audience**. Whether
these questions will define the field's future is open to doubt: there is
little consensus on describing the universal audience, and empirical evidence
shows fields cooperating on specific topics in concrete contexts.
Border-spanning discourse often rests on regional, temporary achievements of
common ground, and case studies may undercut abstract distinctions — inside
versus outside, local versus universal — or lead scholars to regard them as
rhetorical stances (Lyne). If field-invariance is itself a rhetorical
achievement, then it is endemic to contexts, subjects, and audiences — and
argumentation scholarship may turn increasingly to social-scientific and
rhetorical case studies.
