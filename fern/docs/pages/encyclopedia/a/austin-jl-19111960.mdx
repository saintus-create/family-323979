<Lead>
English ordinary-language philosopher and founder of speech act theory. Of
J.L. Austin's work, the aspect most influential for rhetoric is what became
known after his death as "speech-act theory."
</Lead>

## How to do things with words

The key text is the posthumously published *How to Do Things with Words*, a
collection of the William James Lectures Austin gave at Harvard in 1955. His
central insight: the approach to language dominant in philosophy since Plato
was one-sided in its focus on how language refers to and represents the world.
**Words do things as well as refer to things**, and a full theory of language
needs to explain what words do and how we do things with them.

Austin begins by defining the **performative** — a sentence that performs an
action rather than describing one: "I bet you," "I do" (at a marriage
ceremony), "I declare war" (when said by a head of state). He initially
defined this class in opposition to **constative** sentences — "Jack and Joe
made a bet," "Jack and Jill got married," "Great Britain declared war on Nazi
Germany yesterday." Constatives are true or false; performatives achieve their
goal or do not. The distinction has been widely influential — Austin's
*performative* now has a life of its own as roughly a (more positive) synonym
for rhetoric.

## From two classes of sentence to three acts

As *How to Do Things with Words* shows, Austin came to think the
constative/performative distinction too crude to stand up to sustained
examination. He realized he was analyzing **utterances, not sentences** — the
same words can have remarkably different effects in different contexts. All
sentences have performative aspects inasmuch as they all do something, and
what they do depends on context. Utterances are constative and performative
simultaneously, and the class of utterances that are actions is not limited to
explicit performatives: **all utterances are acts — speech acts**.

In making an utterance one performs, Austin said, three acts simultaneously:

1. **Locutionary** — the act of saying something.
2. **Illocutionary** — the act performed *in* saying those words.
3. **Perlocutionary** — the act achieved *by* saying those words.

<Example>
Telling a group of children that a car is coming down the street: I state
something (locutionary), I warn them to be careful (illocutionary), and I
(hope to) alarm them if they are in the road (perlocutionary).
</Example>

## Why it matters for rhetoric

For rhetoricians, the importance of Austin's work is not so much the details
of his analysis — disputed, revised, and elaborated by subsequent speech-act
theorists such as John Searle and by the linguistic movement known as
pragmatics. Far more important is the broad notion of language implicit in the
analysis: **language is a mode of acting in the world, not just a mode of
reflecting on it.** To say, as Austin does, that to speak is to act is to
adopt a deeply rhetorical vision of language.
