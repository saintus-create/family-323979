Commonly, the individual who publishes writing and is in that sense a
professional writer. In legal, literary, and rhetorical theory, *author* has a
more complicated and problematic status — the three disciplines provide
distinctive yet intersecting and complementary perspectives, and most
published treatments examine the concept across all three.

## The legal perspective

From the legal perspective, the author is the person (or persons) assigned
intellectual credit for a produced work, who "owns" the text as intellectual
property (the publisher typically owns the work as legal or physical
property). Intellectual property becomes complicated when the author is a
group of writers or editors, or a conglomeration — a corporation or government
can function as author — or when derivative forms of a text are produced.
Copyright law and fair-use conventions help define authorship and direct
writers who copy, distribute, or otherwise use published work; the law aims to
balance authors' rights to credit for their labors against society's right to
benefit from the free exchange of ideas. Disciplinary citation practices have
developed as ethical procedures for acknowledging intellectual property
rights.

## The literary and rhetorical perspectives

Literary and rhetorical theory has been less interested in ownership than in
the relationship of author to text — developing practices for producing text
(rhetoric) or interpreting it (literary theory). Here *author* becomes
entangled with text-based principles such as ethos, persona, and voice; with
other agent constructs (writer, professional writer, poet, novelist); and with
postmodern issues of identity, subject formation, authority, and presence.

In the Romantic tradition of criticism as it evolved in the twentieth century,
the author was not simply any published writer but a special writer whose
works achieved superior value as aesthetic objects — and could thus be
assigned canonical status. From this perspective, not all writers, not even
all published writers, deserve the title. Indeed, much criticism still
presupposes the author as the unifying presence of the literary work.

Various strands of contemporary criticism challenge author as a foundational
concept or unified authoritative voice, emphasizing instead the writer as
scribe, compiler, or commentator — recalling an earlier Christian and medieval
notion of writer-as-instrument (of an ultimate originating Author, God). The
principle of intertextuality raises questions about the "originality" of any
writing: if all writing is a process of social meaning making, writers are not
original creators so much as collaborators in a wide-area textual discussion.
Social rhetorics view the writer as a discussant rather than a solitary voice
of inspired genius — constructed by texts as well as by audiences.

## A historically recent concept

The author is a fairly recent concept historically — born perhaps in
*Donaldson v. Becket* (1774) — with significant connections to social,
technological, and economic developments. The printing press (a means of
standardizing, and thereby stabilizing, texts) influenced the modern
conception, but it did not fully emerge until ownership of texts became a
legal issue in the eighteenth century, growing out of laissez-faire
capitalism's argument that individuals must be credited for their labor to
motivate it — whether authors seeking monetary capital or, later, student
writers seeking grades and course credit.

## The critique and its aftermath

In conventional literary theory, the author concept stabilizes the text,
invests it with a unifying central authority, and provides stability across a
corpus produced under the same signature. **Poststructuralism** views such a
move as an effort to control text and textual meaning — and thereby knowledge
and intellectual power — inhibiting conflict, contradiction, and revolution;
it valorizes the lone individual voice: a male voice, according to feminist
theorists, and a Western logocentric one according to cultural critics. The
concept is also critiqued for presupposing a linear "information transfer"
model of communication — an author in possession of meaning who installs it in
a text that carries it to the reader.

Other contemporary developments further challenge conventional authorship:

- **Feminist theory** encourages writing practices promoting a plurality of
  voices — a dialogic rather than monologic model.
- **Collaboration research** in rhetoric and composition has challenged "the
  myth of the solitary writer" (Lunsford and Ede 73), establishing how
  frequent and typical collaborative writing is.
- **Computer writing technologies** expose the inadequacies of print notions
  of authorship — particularly in electronic publishing, where interactive
  hypertext lets users construct their own documents along chosen reading
  paths, eroding the boundary between author and audience.

It remains to be seen whether the concept of author has outlived its
usefulness — is it dead, as Roland Barthes claimed? — or whether it can
survive (though no doubt extensively revised) the challenges of postmodern
theory and practice. Certainly in rhetoric theory, and increasingly in
literary theory, the "writer" has supplanted the "author."
