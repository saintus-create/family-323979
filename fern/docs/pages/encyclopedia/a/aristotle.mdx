## Life: between Macedonia and Athens

Aristotle was born in 384 B.C.E. in Stagira in Chalcidice, a much-disputed
territory of northern Greece bordering Macedonia. His father, Nicomachus, a
member of the sacred medical cult of the Asclepiads, was court physician to
the Macedonian king Amyntas III. Nicomachus died in Aristotle's youth, and in
367 Aristotle was sent by his guardian to Athens. Tradition has it that he
left expressly to study at Plato's Academy — but his departure also coincided
with Amyntas's death and the political turmoil over the succession, and
historians generally agree that his identification with the royal court
placed him in some danger. He likely left Chalcidice as much to escape
personal harm as to pursue his education.

Plato and his Academy were heavily involved in the politics of Athens and the
surrounding territories. Although Aristotle's political alliances would differ
from Plato's, he shared the assumption that the philosopher's best function
was to advise rulers — a belief also held by Isocrates, who led the Academy's
rival school. Philodemus reports that Aristotle began lecturing on rhetoric
some ten years after his arrival, and Diogenes Laertius echoes the report that
he did so because "it would be a base and shameful thing to remain silent"
while Isocrates spoke out.

The two differed sharply. Isocrates made no distinction between his art of
discourse and the knowledge he called *philosophia*; Aristotle made careful
distinctions among metaphysics, physics, ethics, and rhetoric — a division
already at work in fragments of his exoteric treatise *Protrepticus*, designed
to set the Academy's goals apart from Isocrates' objectives. Yet both envisioned
themselves as advisers to a ruling class, and both advocated submitting Athens
to Philip of Macedon. The easiest way to distinguish them: Isocrates was a
teacher and rhetor whose political influence ran through the circulation of
written speeches; Aristotle was a philosopher who compiled rhetorical
doctrine and gave rhetoric a formal disciplinary account, his influence
residing in personal counsel.

Aristotle left the Academy after Plato's death in 348–47 — often attributed
to anger over the appointment of Plato's nephew Speusippus, but more likely
to escape reprisals as anti-Macedonian feeling rose in Athens (Philip had
ravaged Olynthus, and Demosthenes was urging war). Aristotle went to the court
of the tyrant Hermias at Assos, apparently as Philip's emissary; a secret
agreement eventually shifted Assos' alliance from Persia to Macedonia. There
he married Hermias' niece and adopted daughter, Pythias. After stays in Assos
and Mytilene, he returned to Macedonia in 343–42.

He is frequently identified as tutor to Philip's son Alexander, but historians
concur that he was likely only one of many tutors — and speculation has it
that he was being groomed to return the Academy's allegiance to Philip. After
Philip's murder in 336, Aristotle returned to Athens in 335 with the full
support of the king. A sacred grove dedicated to Apollo was assigned for his
school; with elaborate facilities and an extraordinary library, the Lyceum
soon became distinguished for the breadth of its studies — philosophical
dialectic, ethics, politics, all the known natural sciences, and rhetoric. The
"Peripatetic" school, named for the covered colonnades where lectures were
held, became the seat of Hellenistic learning, educating the sons of ruling
classes throughout the Hellenistic world.

His fortunes stayed tied to Macedonia's. Alexander's death in 323 rekindled
Athenian hatred of Macedonia, and Diogenes Laertius reports that Aristotle was
formally charged with sacrilege. Before he could be tried, he turned the
Lyceum over to his longtime assistant Theophrastus and left Athens for the
last time, settling at Chalcis in Euboea — secure Macedonian territory and his
mother's homeland. He died one year later, in 322.

## The corpus and Aristotelian philosophy

Like Plato, Aristotle presented his philosophy in dialogue form; only fragments
survive. Our knowledge rests on a corpus generally considered a combination of
notes and lecture records, edited and organized by Andronicus Rhodius, a
first-century B.C.E. philosopher of the surviving Peripatetic school. Scholars
speculate that Andronicus' edition may include the thought of successive
Peripatetics, and many surviving texts were certainly excluded from it.

Aristotelian thought has so permeated two millennia of inquiry that
philosophical, ethical, and political theories continue to define themselves
by their convergence with or divergence from Aristotle — a use that has often
transformed a corpus of inquiry into dogma. It is important to bear in mind
that the surviving corpus is a **record of inquiry, not a closed system of
knowledge**. Relative consistency appears in two areas: the teleological
system and the taxonomy of knowledge.

**Teleology.** Throughout the corpus, inquiry is described in terms of
causality, purpose, and ends (*telos*). Aristotle insists there are many types
of causes — most familiarly his four: **final** (health is the final cause of
medicine), **material** ("that out of which" a thing comes into being — bronze
for the statue), **efficient** (the doctor is the efficient cause of health),
and **formal** (the shape, archetype, or "definition of the essence" — the
formal cause of a musical scale as the ratio 2:1). His conceptions of
**potentiality** (*dunamis*, the capacity to act or be acted upon) and
**actuality** (*energeia*, the movement toward fulfillment,
*entelecheia*) are closely related: the house is a potentiality in the
process of building; once complete, that potentiality is no longer there.

**The taxonomy of knowledge.** Three domains are demarcated by their
relationship to an end:

| Domain | End | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Theoretical (*epistêmê*) | First (formal) principles; pursued for no practical or economic end | Metaphysics, natural sciences, mathematics |
| Practical (*praxis*) | Action; bringing about *eudaimonia* — "happiness" or "the good life" | Ethics, politics |
| Productive (*poesis*) | "Making" — ends always "outside itself," in the use made of the construct by a receiver or audience | *Technê*, or art: medicine, architecture, military strategy, seafaring, poetics, **rhetoric** |

## Aristotle's conception of rhetoric

The *Rhetoric* was not his only treatise on the subject: Cicero mentions a
compilation of rhetorical precepts known as the *Synagoge Technon*, and a lost
early dialogue, the *Gryllus*. Our earliest surviving text of the *Rhetoric*
dates from the tenth century. Despite our dependence on it, Aristotle would
hardly have characterized himself as a rhetor: responding to the popularity of
Isocrates' school, fourth-century curricula had to include rhetoric. George
Kennedy notes that parts of the text are drawn from other inquiries
(psychology in particular), not adapted to the needs of rhetors, and were
likely addressed to students of dialectic. Yet the *Rhetoric* remains a
singular synthesis of systematized precepts and discussions of rhetoric's
ethical, epistemological, and disciplinary character — an exhaustive
collection encompassing discourse classification, invention, modes of proof,
arrangement, and style.

**Classification.** Discourse is classified by its particular *telos*:
deliberative (exhortation or dissuasion) toward the advantageous or harmful;
judicial or forensic (accusation or defense) toward the just or unjust;
demonstrative or epideictic (praise and blame) toward the honorable or
shameful.

**Invention.** One of the more complete ancient treatments. Three kinds of
topics: the *koina* (common to all subjects: possible/impossible, past and
future fact, degree of magnitude), the twenty-eight common topoi (not
restricted to particular subjects — for which reason many scholars assign them
an inventional or heuristic function, though debate persists over whether they
facilitate judgment or provide proof for a thesis already in hand), and
special topics (*idia*), specific to particular contexts and types of proof,
such as appeals to emotion.

**Proof.** Proofs divide into nonartistic (*atechnic*) and artistic
(*technic*), with rhetoric primarily concerned with inventing the latter. The
three major artistic proofs are based on character ([Ethos](/e/ethos)), reason
([Logos](/l/logos)), and emotion ([Pathos](/p/pathos)). Aristotle's discussion
of probable argumentation and the [Enthymeme](/e/enthymeme) remains
foundational, though its function — postjudgment proof or heuristic — is
variously interpreted. What is clear is that he carefully distinguished the
probable reasoning of the rhetorical enthymeme from the rigorous reasoning of
the dialectical syllogism.

**Arrangement and style.** Book III describes the two essential parts of a
speech as statement and proof, with discussions of introductions, narrations,
and conclusions; under style it analyzes [Metaphor](/m/metaphor) and qualities
such as appropriateness, concreteness, and vividness.

**Rhetoric's status.** The entry closes on the debate over rhetoric's
epistemological and ethical character. William M.A. Grimaldi's recent
commentary maintains that rhetoric's province encompasses both philosophy and
ethics — contra Edward Meredith Cope's nineteenth-century view that rhetoric
sits closer to politics and ethics, hence practical knowledge. But Aristotle's
characterization is consistent with his treatment of **productive knowledge**:
the capacity of productive knowledge to deal with the indeterminate — what can
be "otherwise" — is applied directly to rhetoric, whose subjects must "present
us with alternative possibilities" (1357.a.5). If rhetoric is productive
knowledge, it is a uniquely contingent kind of knowledge, subject neither to
the epistemological requirements of theoretical knowledge nor to the ethical
constraints of practical knowledge.
