---
title: "Atticism"
subtitle: "The postclassical preference for the 'pure' style of classical Athens"
---

<Lead>
Appropriated in Latin as a loan-word from Greek (*attikismos*). In its
simplest sense, Atticism describes an attribute of written language akin to —
if not identical with — elegance (Quintilian 1.8.8) and the redolence of the
genuine flavor of things Athenian (Quintilian 6.3.107). Its more common
application, however, was as a literary preference among post-classical
critics for a simple and pure style of language — particularly in oratory —
as opposed to the defective style of "Asianism," a term designating perceived
excesses of emotional expression and stylistic floridity.
</Lead>

## Pure style as archaizing gesture

As Kennedy points out (1963: 330), this valorization of a "pure" style — as
evinced in the "best" prose of classical Athens — is an archaizing gesture
linking Atticism closely with classicism and the canonization of literary
genres. Certain authors were singled out: the quintessentially Attic author
would be someone like Lysias.

## Asianism and its roots

Asianism, insofar as it is a historically recognizable phenomenon, had its
roots in the training offered during the postclassical period at the rhetorical
schools of Asia Minor (in cities such as Pergamum) and on the island of
Rhodes. The objection to Asianism — particularly in Rome — may have had to do
with suspicion and deprecation of all things Eastern, proverbially associated
with luxury, softness, decadence, excess, and intemperance. Greek-speaking
contributors to the debate included Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Caecilius
of Calacte, whose work survives only in fragments.

## Greek Atticism versus the Roman debate

Greek Atticism was primarily a written phenomenon: strict adherence to a
carefully circumscribed vocabulary and to the syntactical patterns favored by
the canonical authors of the fifth and fourth centuries B.C.E. On the Roman
side, the discussion was not merely a scrutiny of antique models — it was
heavily inflected by heated debate over the characteristics appropriate to
spoken oratory in the contemporary civic arena. The principal name here is
Cicero himself, who in the *Brutus* and *Orator* defends his own oratorical
style against charges of Asianism. (It would be interesting to inspect the
oratory of C. Licinius Calvus, touted as a paragon of pure Attic style, and of
Q. Hortensius Hortalus, notoriously Asianic — but their work is known only
from oblique references in Cicero and other writers.)

## Afterlife

The concern for Atticism in Greek writing continued well into the Byzantine
period — comparable to the "Ciceronianism" sometimes insisted upon in the
European Renaissance: the slavish devotion to a Latin prose style closely
approximating Cicero's, using (wherever possible) vocabulary attested in his
extant works.
